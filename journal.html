WEEK 1: SEPT 14th

For my first journal, I read the article on CAN YOU SEE ME NOW, an online game that interfaces directly with the real world. In a way it was similar to Pokemon Go. The game uses networks to place the players in a virtual city mapped onto their actual location. They use movement to explore the city, meet each other and avoid "runners" (other players trying to find and knock them out of the game). This game takes so much advantage of the platform and very well exemplifies the type of things I wish to explore in CART 351. I have always been interested in unconventional games that take advantage of lesser-known internet capabilities such as interactive networks and locational gaming. Pokemon Go was the first success of this on a large stage and is an interesting case study because it created an interesting divide in the community: people who worked together and used the platform to its fullest, and people who tried their very hardest to avoid going outside and using the platform's interactivity with the world. Nowadays it is rare to find a pokemon Go player that doesn't just set their location somewhere else to go where they need for a certain pokemon and/or modify their devices to simulate movement. I wish to create something that exists purely to spite these lazy players and offers so much more to those who are willing to look for it.

WEEK 2: SEPT 21st

This week I refreshed my memory on Python through our first class exercise. I haven't had a lot of experience with Python but I played a game that really sparked my interest in it a while back. It's a small game called "The Farmer Was Replaced", in which you code a drone via python to plant, irrigate, and harvest certain plants. Each plant grew a certain way more optimally and was created to teach the player how to solve different logistical problems using Python, such as a pumpkin that combines when it is near other pumpkins or having to create a sorting algorithm to efficiently harvest cacti. It was a lot of fun and I'm hoping this class strikes my interest in Python again, as I've never done anything network related and I'm excited to try.

WEEK 3: SEPT 28th

This week, I explored dead drops, a project by Aram Bartholl where one would install USB flash drives into the wall in random locations. Users would be encouraged to take and drop files as they wished, and I personally zeroed in on a certain comment on the article detailing the Dead Drops: "This is the nerd equivalent of glory holes". I'm assuming we all know what glory holes are, and so imagine my surprise when I saw that this comment was from 2012. It obviously made sense, given that this is a very 2010's internet sort of art installment, but it was funny to me that people were beginning to imagine the internet as extensions of real-life traditions since then. Since reading this article, I have considered buying a couple of flash drives and putting them up in random places around my neighborhood, though with more attention to the connotation of "tech glory holes". I believe specifically that a dead drop could be a sort of technological speak-easy within smaller communities and taboo relations for free sharing of files without risk of data leaks. It would be an interesting experiment.
